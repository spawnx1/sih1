How to read this manual

Front matter

This is the single book that explains the entire Cash-out Forecast Console — from the human problem it solves, down to the last library in the stack and the last panel on the screen. It assumes no prior knowledge. If you have never seen the code, start at page one and keep going; by the end you will be able to run it, extend it, and defend every design decision to a technical judge.

The manual is written in ten parts. Parts I–II give you the whole idea and a map of the system. Parts III–V descend into the three technical pillars: the data, the money-tracing graph, and the machine-learning engine. Part VI is the most important part for credibility — it explains the six rules that stop the system from cheating, and how each rule is mechanically enforced. Parts VII–VIII cover the officer-facing product: the decision rules, the alert system, and the command console. Parts IX–X tell you how to run it, demo it, and where it goes next.

Reading paths

The presenter / judge-facing team: Parts I, VI, and the demo script (Ch. 39) are enough to stand up and explain the project convincingly.
The engineer joining the team: read straight through; every module is mapped to its file.
The person in a hurry: a separate 15-page Field Guide distils this whole book into the essentials.

Throughout, colour-coded boxes flag three things: red boxes are the non-negotiable rules that keep the science honest; amber boxes are cautions and things people get wrong; green boxes are the key takeaways worth memorising.

What this project is

Part I · Orientation

When someone is scammed online, the fraudster does not keep the money. Within minutes they move it through a chain of rented "mule" bank accounts and then pull it out as cash at an ATM. Once it is cash, it is effectively unrecoverable. This system takes a fraud complaint, reconstructs where the money went, and then forecasts — for each account still holding the stolen money — whether it will be cashed out, when, through what channel, and roughly at which ATM. The purpose is to freeze the account before the cash is withdrawn, and, if that is impossible, to tell police where to look.

The one-sentence pitch

"We don't just detect fraud after the fact — we forecast the cash-out, so police can freeze the account in time, and when they can't, we tell them which area to watch."

That sentence contains the entire thesis. Every design decision in the codebase exists to make that forecast accurate, fast enough to act on, and honest about its own uncertainty. Those three properties — accuracy, timeliness, and honesty — are the axes on which the project should be judged, and they are the reason the architecture looks the way it does.

Where it fits

The prototype is built against the workflow of the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) under the Ministry of Home Affairs, and the national complaint system (the citizen files a report; a fourteen-digit acknowledgement number, the ack_no, identifies the case). The console is designed to sit on an investigating officer's desk: a queue of live complaints on the left, a forecast and a live money-flow map in the centre, and a set of concrete actions — freeze this account, dispatch to this station, keep tracing — on the right.

What "cash-out forecasting" means precisely

It is not transaction fraud scoring (is this payment fraudulent?). The fraud has already happened. It is a forward-looking, time-boxed prediction about the fate of already-stolen money: given the money is now sitting in account X, what is the probability it becomes untraceable cash in the next 10, 20, 30, 45, or 60 minutes, and where.

2 · The problem, in real terms

Part I · Orientation

To understand why the system is shaped the way it is, you have to understand the timeline of a real cash-out, because the whole design is a race against that clock.

The anatomy of a cash-out

  1. The fraud. A victim is tricked into sending money — a fake KYC update, a "your account is blocked" call, a bogus refund. One transaction leaves their account. This is the seed transaction.
  2. Layering. The money does not sit still. Within seconds to minutes it is split and forwarded through several mule accounts. Splitting it makes it harder to trace and spreads the withdrawal across many people and ATMs. This is the classic money-laundering layering step, compressed into minutes.
  3. Cash-out. Each mule withdraws their share as cash at a local ATM (or a POS "cash-at-till"). The instant it is cash, the paper trail ends.

The brutal arithmetic of time

The reason this is hard — and the reason a forecast is valuable rather than a report — is the mismatch between two delays:

  • The forwarding delay (fraud → cash) is typically minutes.
  • The reporting delay (fraud → complaint filed) is typically around 110 minutes.

The honest, uncomfortable fact

In roughly two out of three complaints, the money was already cashed out before the complaint was even filed. A prototype that pretends otherwise is lying. This system is built to be useful in the cases where action is still possible, and — just as importantly — to make the case for the two structural fixes that would widen that window: faster reporting (auto-alerts at the bank) and proactive freezing.

This single fact reframes the goal. The winning move is not "catch them at the ATM" — by the time you know, the cash is gone. The winning move is "freeze the account before the ATM." Freezing is a database action that takes seconds; a police dispatch takes tens of minutes. So the system optimises for the thing that can actually beat the clock: a fast, well-justified freeze recommendation, routed to the right desk, with a geographic fallback for when freezing fails.

3 · The big idea

Part I · Orientation

Most fraud tooling is retrospective: it labels transactions as fraudulent so they can be investigated later. That is necessary but it does not save the money. This project makes a deliberate bet on being prospective: it treats the stolen money as a moving object and forecasts its next state.

The consequence of that bet runs all the way down. Because we forecast the future, every feature must be computed from the past only (Part IV/VI). Because officers must act on the forecast, the output is not a probability in isolation but a tiered recommendation with a reason (Part VII). And because a false "go to ATM #14" wastes a patrol, the location model is allowed to abstain — to say "I only know the neighbourhood" — rather than bluff (Chapter 24). Honesty is not decoration here; it is a functional requirement.

5 · The five-step pipeline

Part II · The system at a glance

The whole system is a pipeline of five stages. A complaint comes in; the money is traced; each holding account is forecast; plain rules turn forecasts into decisions; and alerts go out. Everything else in this manual is a detailed view of one of these five boxes.

Two properties of this pipeline are worth stating up front. First, it is rebuilt fresh for every prediction — there is no giant precomputed graph of the whole world sitting around, because that would be the first and worst way to leak future information. Second, the intelligence is deliberately concentrated in stages 3 and 4 and kept out of the plumbing: the decision layer (stage 4) is plain, readable rules, not a black box, so an officer can always read why an alert fired.

7 · Architecture & data flow

Part II · The system at a glance

The system is a single Python backend that serves a single-file web console. There is no database server, no message queue, no cloud dependency — it runs entirely on one machine from a folder of Parquet files and trained model artefacts. That simplicity is a feature: the whole thing is reproducible and auditable.

The offline half (left) runs a handful of times: it generates the synthetic world, trains the three models, and produces the evaluation report. Its outputs are just files — Parquet tables under data/ and joblib model files under models/. The online half (centre) is the FastAPI server: on startup it loads everything into memory as a FeatureContext, and thereafter every request is answered by re-tracing the trail and re-running the models as of the requested moment. The console (right) is a single HTML file served as a static asset; it talks to the backend purely over JSON.

10 · Why the data is synthetic

Part III · The data foundation

Every row of data in this project is generated by our own code. That is not a shortcut — it is a legal necessity and, handled correctly, a scientific strength. This chapter explains both, because "your data is fake" is the first thing a sceptic will say, and there is a strong answer.

The legal reason

Real money-mule chains are drawn from live bank and complaint records. In India those are personal financial data protected under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. They cannot lawfully be poured into an open prototype that students build and judges inspect. A synthetic world is the only responsible way to build and share this system.

The scientific reason it still counts

A synthetic dataset is only worthless if it is easy — if mules and honest customers look obviously different, a model "learns" nothing transferable. Our generator is built to be the opposite: deliberately hard. Mules and legitimate customers overlap heavily, so the model must learn subtle, genuine signal.

The realism levers (why the lessons transfer)

Overlapping mule and legitimate timing distributions · six legitimate archetypes that look like mules (a salaried worker withdrawing their salary, a small business making round-number deposits, a student with a fresh account) · partial observability (some inter-bank edges are simply unobserved, as in reality) · injected label noise · and a mule "ring" structure that shares ATMs. A model that scores well on this world is learning behaviour, not a watermark.

11 · The synthetic world

Part III · The data foundation

The generator (gen/generator.py) builds a self-consistent little economy and then lets fraud happen inside it. The pieces:

Accounts (8,000)

Each account has a KYC-registered district and PIN (the only location the models may use), an opening branch, an opening date (so account age is a real feature), and a hidden archetype that drives its behaviour. About 6% are mules. The archetype is never shown to the models — it lives only in a sealed internal table.

Archetypes — the overlap that makes it hard

Legitimate (94%)Mule (6%)
SALARIED · SMB · STUDENT · SENIOR · FARMER — each with realistic, and deliberately mule-like, transaction rhythms.MULE_FRESH (just recruited) · MULE_SEASONED (established) · MULE_RING (operated as a coordinated set).

Transactions (~250,000)

Ordinary economic activity — salaries, purchases, transfers, ATM withdrawals — plus the fraudulent flows. Every transaction has a timestamp, amount, channel, and an observed flag (some inter-bank edges are unobserved, modelling imperfect data sharing). Cash-out channels are ATM withdrawals and POS.

Complaints (600)

Each complaint names one victim and one seed transaction, records a filing time (with a realistic reporting delay), a disputed amount, and a fraud category. These 600 cases are what the console's queue is built from.

Rings (size 3)

Mules are grouped into small rings that share cash-out ATMs and coordinate. This is the single most useful fraud-specific signal, and it is why one of the five candidate-ATM sources (C4, Ch. 19) looks at what an account's ring co-members have done.

If any module under ml/ or graphx/ read a generator parameter, the model would be told the answer instead of learning it. The leakage test greps imports and fails the build if this boundary is crossed. The only legitimate readers are the generator itself and the evaluation harness (to build the sealed shift).

ATMs

gen/atms.py scatters ~700 synthetic ATM terminals across these districts. Each terminal has an operator (bank), a PIN, a district, and — crucially — its H3 cells at two resolutions. The file also carries the exact OpenStreetMap Overpass query to swap in real amenity=atm nodes when the pilot goes live.

H3 — the spatial backbone

H3 is Uber's hexagonal grid index. A latitude/longitude collapses to a hexagon id at a chosen resolution. We use two:

  • Resolution 7 (~5 km²) — the operational cell. This is the neighbourhood-sized area the location model reports when it is confident about the area but not the exact ATM.
  • Resolution 8 (~0.5 km²) — the fine tier, a tighter grouping used inside the location features.

H3 is what makes the location hierarchy — district → cell → terminal — clean and computable: aggregating terminal probabilities up to their parent cell is just a group-by on the cell id.

14 · The sealed corpus

Part III · The data foundation

The most common attack on any model trained on self-generated data is: "your model just memorised your own generator." The sealed corpus is the pre-built answer.

Params.sealed() returns a deliberately different distribution, and the model is evaluated on data built from it without ever training on it. The differences are structural, not cosmetic:

  • a different mule-hotspot mix (fraud clusters in different districts);
  • slower forwarding delays (the money moves at a different pace);
  • lower fraud prevalence (rarer positives);
  • worse bank cooperation (more unobserved edges);
  • and a fraud typology — smish_burst — that never appears in training at all.

The payoff

Performance on the sealed corpus barely drops (ROC-AUC 0.913 → 0.879). A memoriser would collapse; a model that learned genuine behaviour degrades gracefully. That single before/after number is the strongest evidence in the whole project that the learning is real.

All datetimes are stored as naive objects understood to be IST. The +05:30 offset appears in exactly one place: JSON serialisation. This keeps the pipeline from ever juggling timezone-aware objects, which is a classic source of subtle bugs.

16 · The as-of principle

Part IV · Tracing the money

This is the single most important idea in the entire project. Everything that makes the results credible flows from it. If you take one concept away from this manual, take this one.

A forecast is a statement about the future. If, while computing it, you accidentally use any information that only exists after the moment of the forecast, you have cheated — you have "peeked at the answer." Your accuracy will look spectacular in testing and collapse in the real world. This mistake is called data leakage, and it is the number-one reason machine-learning prototypes fail when deployed.

R1 — the as-of rule

Every feature in this system is a function f(account_id, as_of, ctx) and may read only data with ts < as_of. The graph is rebuilt fresh at every as_of. There is no precomputed whole-corpus graph — that would be the first and worst leak.

Enforcing this is not a matter of good intentions — humans forget. It is enforced mechanically (Ch. 27): the leakage test rebuilds a physically truncated corpus (all future rows deleted) and asserts that features computed on it are bit-for-bit identical to features computed with the as-of filter on the full corpus. If a feature ever peeks, the two disagree and the build fails.

17 · Money-flow reconstruction

Part IV · Tracing the money

graphx/trail.py turns "one victim, one seed transaction, one moment" into the graph of where the money went. It is a breadth-first walk outward from the seed, and it obeys the as-of rule at every step.

The algorithm

  1. Start at the seed edge: victim → first mule, fully tainted.
  2. From each account, follow only outgoing edges whose timestamp lies strictly between the money's arrival and as_of (delegated to the feature store so the filter is applied identically everywhere).
  3. Ignore dust: edges worth less than 5% of the seed amount (MIN_EDGE_FRACTION) are dropped.
  4. Attribute taint proportionally through each split: if an account received ₹100 of stolen money and forwards ₹60 of a ₹300 total outflow, the tainted portion of that edge is scaled accordingly, capped at the edge amount.
  5. Stop at depth 4 (MAX_HOPS) or when a cash-out ends the chain.

What comes out

A Trail of nodes and edges. A node is flagged is_leaf when it received more than it forwarded — i.e. it is currently holding stolen funds. Those leaves are the forecast targets: the accounts a freeze could still save. Each node also carries its KYC centroid (lat/lon/district/pin) so the console can plot the flow, and its total tainted amount so the decision layer can size the risk.

Why rebuild every time?

Because the trail as-of 10:05 is a different graph from the trail as-of 10:25 — more money has moved. A precomputed graph would silently contain future edges. Rebuilding is cheap (the BFS is capped and pruned) and it is the only way to keep R1 honest.

18 · Graph features & the mule score

Part IV · Tracing the money

graphx/features.py computes nine numbers per node that capture where in the fraud structure an account sits — signal you cannot get from an account's own transactions alone. All nine are as-of correct.

#Graph featureIntuition

2in_deg_1hIncoming edges in the last hour — a mule receiving from many.
3out_deg_1hOutgoing edges in the last hour — fast fan-out.

The mule score is a feature, not a headline

Feature 9 is not a separate model competing with M1. It is one column that M1 consumes. Keeping it as a feature (rather than a standalone classifier) means its uncertainty is folded into the hazard model honestly, and it stays as-of correct like everything else.

19 · Candidate ATM generation

Part IV · Tracing the money

The location model (M3) does not score all 700 ATMs — that would be slow and mostly noise. Instead, graphx/candidates.py first proposes a shortlist of plausible ATMs for an account, and M3 only ranks those. The shortlist is the union of five sources, in priority order, capped at 50 (CANDIDATE_CAP):

Source~nWhere the candidates come from
C115Every ATM this account itself used in the last ~90–120 days (its own habit).
C212ATMs within 4 km of the account's historical withdrawal centroid.
C310ATMs within 3 km of its KYC PIN centroid (falls back to the opening branch).
C410The differentiator: top ATMs used by the account's ring co-members in 180 days.
C58The district's top fraud-withdrawal ATMs — train period only.

Candidate recall is the ceiling

If the real ATM is not in the shortlist, no ranker can ever find it. So candidate recall@50 — the fraction of cases where the true ATM is somewhere in the shortlist — is a hard ceiling on location accuracy. Ours is 0.82, and source C4 (the ring signal) is the single biggest contributor. This is why modelling the ring mattered.

20 · The as-of feature store

Part V · The forecasting engine

The FeatureContext in ml/features.py is the workhorse that makes as-of feature computation both correct and fast. Understanding it is understanding how the leakage rule is kept without making the system unusably slow.

On startup, the context loads all transactions once and pre-sorts them per account by timestamp. Then any as-of query — "this account's withdrawals before 10:05" — is answered with a binary search (numpy.searchsorted) that finds the cut-point in a sorted array in microseconds, rather than filtering 250,000 rows every time. The same cut is applied through one code path (_before), so every feature, every model, and the leakage test all slice time identically. That single shared cut is what makes the bit-for-bit leakage guarantee possible.

The context also exposes the small priors the models legitimately use: an ATM's historical fraud rate, a district's hotspot prior, an account's KYC info and ring membership, and helpers like the account's withdrawal centroid and radius of gyration.

The four channel classes

M2 predicts over exactly four outcomes: ATM, POS, onward_transfer, dormant. The last two matter operationally: "onward_transfer" tells police not to rush an ATM because the money is about to move again.

22 · M1 — the hazard curve

Part V · The forecasting engine

M1 answers "will this account be cashed out, and when?" It is a discrete-time hazard model: a gradient-boosted classifier scored at each horizon in {10, 20, 30, 45, 60} minutes, with the horizon itself as a feature. The five scores are stitched into a cumulative curve (forced to be non-decreasing — the chance of cash-out within an hour can't be lower than the chance within ten minutes).

From the curve, M1 reads a window (the steepest-rise interval — when the risk is climbing fastest) and a severity label: imminent (≥60% within 10 min), soon (≥30%), later (≥10%), or unlikely. The 10-minute probability, p_10m, is the headline number the decision layer keys on.

23 · M2 — the channel

Part V · The forecasting engine

M2 answers "how will the money leave?" — a four-class classifier over ATM, POS, onward_transfer, and dormant. It exists to stop a wasteful mistake: sending a patrol to an ATM when the money is actually about to be forwarded again (onward_transfer) or is going nowhere right now (dormant). It sharpens the action, not just the probability.

24 · M3 — location & abstention

Part V · The forecasting engine

M3 answers "where?" — and it is the model the project is proudest of, because of how it handles not knowing. It ranks the candidate ATMs from Chapter 19, but then it does something most models refuse to do: it decides at what level of precision it is willing to commit.

R5 — hierarchical abstention

M3 computes the terminal probability distribution and its entropy, then applies a two-gate rule:

· if the top terminal's probability ≥ 0.22 and entropy ≤ 2.60 nats → emit a terminal (the top 5 ATMs);
· else if the top cell's probability ≥ 0.30 → emit a cell, and record that it abstained at terminal;
· else → emit only the district, abstaining at cell.

The output always carries abstained_at and a plain-English abstain_reason, so the officer sees exactly why the system would only name a neighbourhood.

This is the "the system knows what it doesn't know" property. On a flat distribution, naming a single ATM would be a coin-flip dressed up as certainty; abstaining to the cell is both more honest and more useful (a patrol can watch a neighbourhood; it cannot stake out an ATM it was wrongly sent to). Judges consistently reward this because it is the opposite of the usual demo temptation to over-claim.

25 · Training & inference

Part V · The forecasting engine

Training (ml/train.py, ml/panel.py)

ml/panel.py builds the training table: for many (account, as_of) pairs it computes the full as-of feature vector and attaches the true outcome. ml/train.py then fits the three models and the mule-score logistic, and serialises them to models/*.joblib together with their exact feature lists (so inference uses the identical columns in the identical order).

Inference (ml/predict.py)

Given an account and an as_of, inference builds the feature vector once, runs M1 across the horizons to form the hazard curve, runs M2 for the channel, generates candidates and runs M3 with the abstention gates, and returns a single Prediction. This is the object the API hands to the decision layer and the console.

26 · The six non-negotiable rules

Part VI · Honesty & evaluation

This is the part to read twice. Six rules keep the science honest, and — crucially — each is enforced by machinery, not by hoping the team remembers. When a judge probes "how do I know you didn't cheat?", these six answers are the reason the project holds up.

RuleWhat it forbidsHow it is enforced
R1 as-ofUsing any data from after the prediction moment.Every feature is f(id, as_of, ctx); the leakage test rebuilds a truncated corpus and asserts identical features.
R2 label prefixA label reaching a model.drop_leaky_columns strips every label_* column on sight.
R3 no live locationUsing a suspect's real-time GPS/phone location.Only KYC centroid, opening branch, and historical withdrawal footprint are exposed; true home coordinates live in a sealed internal table and are never shipped.
R4 honest metricsSuspiciously high scores that signal a leak.Target ROC-AUC 0.86–0.92; a single-feature audit flags anything scoring >0.85 alone. We widened the generator until 0.913 was true, never tuned upward.
R5 abstentionNaming a precise location on a flat distribution.The district→cell→terminal gates in M3, with abstained_at / abstain_reason.
R6 naive ISTTimezone bugs from mixed tz handling.All datetimes naive IST internally; +05:30 appended only at JSON serialisation.

Why R4 sounds backwards — and is the point

Most teams chase the highest possible accuracy. We capped our ambition at 86–92% and treat any single feature that alone scores above 0.85 as a bug to hunt, because in this domain a too-good number almost always means a leak. Deliberately lowering the ceiling to keep it honest is exactly the discipline a real deployment needs.

27 · The leakage test suite

Part VI · Honesty & evaluation

tests/test_leakage.py is the mechanical conscience of the project. It runs under pytest and turns the rules above into failing builds. Its checks:

  • As-of identity (R1). Build a corpus physically truncated at as_of (all later rows deleted). Compute features on it, and on the full corpus with the as-of filter. Assert they are bit-for-bit identical. Any feature that peeks makes them differ.
  • Label containment (R2). Assert no label_* column survives into a model's input.
  • Location containment (R3). Assert the generator's ground-truth home coordinates never appear in any shipped table or feature.
  • Import boundary. Grep the ml/ and graphx/ trees and fail if any of them import gen/params.py (which would mean reading the answer key).

The one-line pitch for this suite

"We don't ask you to trust that we didn't cheat — we wrote tests that fail the build if we did, and they run on every change."

30 · The plain-rules decision layer

Part VII · Decision & alerts

The models produce probabilities. Turning a probability into an action is done by plain, readable rules — deliberately not another model — because an officer must be able to read exactly why an alert fired, and a court must be able to audit it.

TierRuleAction
REDp_10m ≥ 0.60 AND tainted amount ≥ ₹5,000FREEZE now + alert the station.
AMBER0.30 ≤ p_10m < 0.60Watch / email a brief.
GREYbelow AMBERKeep tracing.

The amount floor is set realistically: after fan-out the money is split across mules, so a single account rarely holds the whole disputed sum — but a 60%-imminent cash-out of even ~₹15,000 is worth a freeze, because freezing is cheap and letting it clear is not. Every recommendation carries a plain-English reason string — the rule trace — so nothing about the verdict is mysterious.

31 · Alert routing & dedup

Part VII · Decision & alerts

When a case fires, an alert is built and logged (SMS / email / webhook payloads). Routing uses the jurisdiction table in config.py: each district maps to a covering cyber police station and its SHO contact, so a Hadapsar cash-out alerts the Hadapsar Cyber PS. To avoid spamming, alerts are de-duplicated: at most one per account per 15 minutes (ALERT_DEDUP_MINUTES).

Simulated, and honest about it

The system builds and logs the notification payloads; it does not actually send them. That is the correct posture for a prototype and is stated plainly — the plumbing is real, the delivery is stubbed.

33 · Design philosophy

Part VIII · The console

The console is one HTML file with no build step. Its job is to make a complex forecast legible to an officer under time pressure, and to make the money move visibly so the story is obvious. The visual language is a calm, mature command-console — dark cartography, restrained accents, and a single clear "what do I do" per case.

An earlier iteration leaned neon; the current theme is deliberately toned down to a muted steel-teal palette with soft glows, so it reads as a serious operations console rather than an arcade. The three accent colours carry fixed meaning everywhere: teal = information, amber = watch, red = act/urgent.

34 · The three views

Part VIII · The console

Command Centre

The situational overview: cases tracked, freeze-now count, money at risk, average window left, and a metro-wide risk map. It answers "what is the state of play right now?" at a glance. A collapsible sidebar lists active cases, most urgent first, each with its risk tier and golden-hour clock.

Case view

The heart of the product for a single case. It shows the live money-flow map (Ch. 35), the ranked target-ATM menu, the hazard/channel/location forecast, the freeze recommendation, and a replay control. Clicking a case loads it here.

Case Centre

The case-management workspace (Ch. 37): the ticket queue, filters, per-ticket detail, and the resolution workflow with an audit trail.

35 · The live money-flow map

Part VIII · The console

The map is Leaflet with a dark Esri canvas base and a labels layer so the officer can read the actual neighbourhoods. Nodes are drawn as clean circular icons: the victim, the mule accounts (the holding mule highlighted), and the ranked candidate ATMs. Money flows are drawn as animated "waypoint" lines: gold for observed flow (victim → mules), red for the predicted routes (holding mule → each candidate ATM, #1 the primary).

The one non-obvious engineering detail worth knowing

The connecting lines are not drawn as ordinary Leaflet polylines. Leaflet paints markers above polylines and, under the large render padding this map uses, its SVG layer can mis-position paths by the padding offset — which made the lines vanish under the icons or off-screen. The fix: the routes are drawn in a dedicated screen-space SVG overlay that sits above the whole map and is redrawn every frame from latLngToContainerPoint — the map's own pixel coordinates. This glues the lines pixel-perfectly to the icons regardless of zoom, pan, or resize. Each route is also trimmed to stop at the edge of each circle (by the node's radius) so the icons and their rank numbers stay clean.

36 · The cinematic replay

Part VIII · The console

The single best demo moment. Pressing Replay simulation re-runs a real case in fast motion inside a cinematic frame: a letterboxed view with a running timecode and act labels ("MONEY ENTERS", "TRACING THE FLOW", "CONVERGING ON CASH-OUT", "CASH-OUT WINDOW — FREEZE"). The camera is held fixed on the whole case so the money reveals systematically; the observed gold flow traces first, and the predicted red target routes reveal only near the climax — mirroring how the real forecast sharpens as the golden hour closes. It turns an abstract probability into a visible, tense story an audience can follow.

37 · The SOAR ticket lifecycle

Part VIII · The console

The Case Centre turns forecasts into managed casework, in the style of a security operations (SOAR) console. Each case becomes a ticket with a priority (P1/P2/P3 derived from the RED/AMBER/GREY tier), a status, an assignee, investigation notes, and a resolution outcome — all backed by a small SQLite store (WAL mode, seeded once). Officers can filter the queue, open a ticket to see its full forecast and audit trail, assign it, add notes, and resolve it (e.g. frozen in time, money recovered). A printable incident report and a CSV export round it out. This is what makes the prototype feel like an operational tool rather than a single-shot demo.

41 · The GNN research track

Part X · Research & roadmap

The shipping models (M1–M3) are gradient-boosted trees — reliable, fast, and easy to defend. Alongside them, the graphx/ tree contains a substantial research track exploring graph neural networks as a future upgrade, built on PyTorch and PyTorch Geometric.

This work is organised as a series of "sprints" and baselines:

  • Graph & hetero baselines — plain and heterogeneous GNN classifiers over the account graph, to test whether learned message-passing beats hand-built graph features.
  • Temporal GNNs (TGNN) — models that respect the as-of ordering and learn from the sequence of movements, not a static snapshot.
  • Candidate-ceiling & ATM-ranker studies — measuring how high location accuracy could go if candidate recall improved, and pairwise-ranking alternatives to the current softmax ranker.
  • Explainability & final validation — sprint 11/12 work on explaining GNN outputs and validating them under the same leakage discipline as the shipping models.

Why trees ship and GNNs wait

For a time-critical, auditable deployment, a boosted-tree model that a court can inspect and that trains in seconds beats a heavier GNN that is harder to explain. The GNN track is the documented path to more accuracy once the operational basics are proven — not a dependency of the current system.

42 · Known limitations

Part X · Research & roadmap

Stated plainly, because credibility comes from naming your own weaknesses first.

  • ATM coordinates are synthetic, scattered around real district centroids; gen/atms.py holds the exact Overpass query to swap in real ATMs.
  • All training data is synthetic by design (DPDP Act). The generator is deliberately hard so the modelling lessons transfer, but it is not real mule data.
  • Not a licensed forecast of any real person or account. Outputs are decision-support for authorised investigators, not evidence.
  • The location ranker is the simple binary-classifier version (softmax within group); a pairwise ranker is a documented next step.
  • Alerts are simulated — payloads are built and logged, not delivered.

43 · Roadmap to production

Part X · Research & roadmap
  1. Real geography. Swap synthetic ATMs for OpenStreetMap amenity=atm nodes (query already in the code).
  2. Bank integration. Wire the freeze recommendation to a real account-freeze API, and the alerts to real SMS/email/webhook gateways.
  3. Faster reporting. The biggest single win is upstream: bank-side auto-alerts that shrink the ~110-minute reporting delay, widening the window the model can act in.
  4. Model upgrades. Promote the temporal GNN track if it beats trees under the same leakage discipline; add the pairwise location ranker.
  5. Hardening. Authentication, per-station access control, and audit logging on every action for evidentiary use.
